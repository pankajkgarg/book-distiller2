# Book map

## Identity

- Title: Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions
- Author: Gary Klein
- Type: applied cognitive psychology / naturalistic decision making
- Test boundary: supplied Chapters 1-3 only

## Argument arc

The test excerpt moves from the book's reversal of conventional decision research, through the field method that exposed a different account of expertise, to the recognition-primed decision model itself. Chapter 1 establishes the domain and the non-analytical “sources of power.” Chapter 2 explains how the researchers' failed assumptions and critical-incident interviews revealed what commanders actually did. Chapter 3 supplies the core model, evidence, variants, limits, and theoretical contrast.

## Chapter ledger

### Chapter 1: Chronicling the Strengths Used in Making Difficult Decisions

- Tier: pivotal.
- Role: frames the book's distinctive question and defines naturalistic decision-making settings.
- Must retain: the reversal from cataloguing human limitations to studying strengths; the torn-artery story and how experience shaped Lieutenant M's rapid choices; intuition, mental simulation, metaphor, and storytelling; all defining features of naturalistic settings and the contrasts with laboratory research; the practical orientation.
- Defining evidence: Lieutenant M's ten-minute rescue; experienced fire commanders and chess masters; ill-defined fireground goals; cue learning; conditions changing repeatedly; team coordination.
- Develop: the field's reversal from deficits to strengths; the torn-artery rescue from the alarm through the ten-minute hospital arrival; the four sources of power; why naturalistic research needs experienced people in realistic conditions.
- Mention: the domains studied; the complete inventory of naturalistic-setting features; time-pressure and experience figures; ill-defined fireground goals; the racetrack cue example; changing conditions; team coordination; practical applications.
- Omit: the unused fire pole, broken-ankle explanation, bikini-clad neighbors, the uneventful overnight garage call, the full contrast for every individual laboratory feature, footnote markers, and the explanation of why some chapters have key-point lists.
- Excerpt anchors: “Instead of trying to show”; “This example shows decision making”; “The power of intuition enables”; “Therefore, subjects are given totally novel tasks”; “If the things we learn”.

### Chapter 2: Learning from the Firefighters

- Tier: supporting.
- Role: shows how a mostly wrong research plan was corrected and how critical-incident interviewing exposed recognition rather than option comparison.
- Must retain: the failed decision aids; the original six assumptions and which failed; Soelberg's justification finding; the commanders' rejection of comparative evaluation; partial rather than whole-incident analogies; the storytelling interview method, paired interviewers, probes, decision points, and conservative analysis.
- Defining evidence: “I don't make decisions”; Chief V's billboard memory; experts' appetite for recounting difficult cases.
- Develop: the failure of prior decision aids; the two-option hypothesis and Soelberg result; the commanders' lack of comparative evaluation; the critical-incident storytelling method and how it found decision points.
- Mention: all six original plan elements and whether each worked; Chief V's partial analogy; interview pairs, probes, recording, and conservative analysis.
- Omit: extended administrative history, repeated statements that the original plan was wrong, and parallel procedural detail that does not change the method.
- Excerpt anchors: “Unfortunately, most of the aids”; “They were not actually making a decision”; “I don't make decisions”; “Rather than waiting for the tough cases”.

### Chapter 3: The Recognition-Primed Decision Model

- Tier: pivotal.
- Role: presents the book's core model and the evidence that experience supports rapid recognition plus singular evaluation through mental simulation.
- Must retain: spongy-roof expertise; laundry-chute fire; overpass rescue; singular versus comparative evaluation; satisficing; mental simulation; Christmas oil-tank fire and the boundary condition of inadequate experience; category counts; all RPD variants, components, evidence, and theoretical implications; individual versus team decision context.
- Defining evidence: all three named incidents; the conservative coding of 156 decision points; the contrast with Janis and Mann.
- Develop: laundry-chute fire; overpass rescue; singular evaluation, satisficing, and mental simulation; Christmas oil-tank fire as boundary condition; all RPD variants and components.
- Mention: spongy-roof expertise; category counts; each conservative safeguard; Janis and Mann contrast; team context.
- Omit: repeated defenses after every distinct safeguard is represented and duplicate restatements that experts recognize rather than compare.
- Excerpt anchors: “To new firefighters”; “It was not that the commanders”; “She slithered through the belt”; “Before we did this study”; “From this episode we learn”; “The recognition-primed decision”.
